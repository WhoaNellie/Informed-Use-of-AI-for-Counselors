Actionable AI Stewardship for Counselors

As artificial intelligence rapidly enters healthcare, counselors have an ethical obligation under the ACA Code of Ethics and MSJCC competencies to maintain technological competence, safeguard client privacy, and actively dismantle algorithmic bias across three ecological tiers of action.

Explore Calls to Action
  1. 01

    Micro Level

    Individual practice: Competence, data privacy, informed consent, bias checks, and cognitive autonomy.

  2. 02

    Mezzo Level

    Agency & Community: Curriculum integration, agency AI audits, advisory boards, and colleague dialogue.

  3. 03

    Macro Level

    Systems & Policy: State/federal licensing governance, transparency legislation, and community risk advocacy.

Micro Level Calls to Action

Actions within your individual counseling practice to maintain technological competence, ensure health data privacy, protect clinical decision-making, and foster client digital self-management.

  • Hana Lee Competence

    Technological Competence & Continuing Ed

    Counselors must maintain professional competence in the safety, security, and transparency of digital health tools (including AI) before adopting them. Follow authoritative sources such as ACA AI Work Group Recommendations and APA's AI Tool Guide (ACA C.2.a, H.1.a).

  • Hana Lee Privacy

    Health Data Privacy & Legal Compliance

    Develop deep competency in health data privacy laws—including WA's My Health My Data Act and HIPAA. Guide clients in selecting and safely using self-guided digital mental health support tools (ACA H.2.d).

  • Nellie Nelson Consent

    Informed Consent & AI Usage Disclosure

    Incorporate explicit informed disclosure regarding AI usage in informed consent documents. Outline potential privacy, security, and diagnostic risks alongside your specific clinical mitigation protocols (ACA A.2.b, Section H).

  • Nellie Nelson Client Care

    Client Guidance & Knowledge Boundaries

    When clients disclose using personal AI tools for self-help, proactively provide peer-reviewed literature, explain machine limitations, address misapprehensions, and clearly emphasize the bounds of your professional knowledge.

  • Scott Robertson Bias Audit

    Critical Evaluation & MSJCC Self-Awareness

    Critically audit AI outputs (session summaries, research, intake notes) for algorithmic bias prior to clinical use. Build self-awareness regarding data origins and burdened populations as part of MSJCC self-awareness commitments (ACA C.5).

  • Scott & Kory Autonomy

    Cognitive Autonomy & Client Literacy

    Limit over-reliance on AI dialogue systems to protect core clinical reasoning skills (especially for Counselors-in-Training). Build client AI literacy as a protective buffer against misinformation and uncritical adoption.

Mezzo Level Calls to Action

Actions within your organization, agency, academic institution, or local professional community to institute oversight, staff education, and equity-centered tool reviews.

  • Hana Lee

    Graduate & CE Curriculum Advocacy

    Advocate for graduate counseling programs and continuing education providers to integrate comprehensive coursework on emerging digital health technologies, machine learning ethics, and data security.

  • Hana Lee

    Interdisciplinary Coalitions

    Join professional advocacy groups and multi-sector coalitions to advance responsible development, clinical deployment, and rigorous oversight across the digital mental health ecosystem.

  • Nellie Nelson

    Institutional AI Audits & Training

    Advocate for mandatory review and risk auditing of all proposed AI software within your agency. Require organizational training on safe, effective, and privacy-compliant usage before adoption.

  • Nellie Nelson

    Ethical AI Advisory Boards

    Establish agency-level Ethical AI Advisory Boards to continuously monitor technology implementations, educate colleagues, and ensure privacy and algorithmic bias risks are actively mitigated.

  • Scott Robertson

    Clinic Equity & Environmental Reviews

    Elevate bias and equity issues to clinic tool reviews: critically evaluate who is represented in training datasets, who bears clinical/financial risk, and the environmental impacts of computing power.

  • Scott Robertson

    Collegial Ethics Dialogue

    Proactively engage colleagues, supervisors, and administrative leaders in regular discussions regarding the ever-evolving ethical and clinical implications of AI dialogue and documentation systems.

Macro Level Calls to Action

Systemic-level advocacy targeting policy, state licensing boards, legislative assemblies, and national professional governance to ensure public safety and equitable technology implementation.

  1. Nellie Nelson · State & National Governance

    Measured Regulatory Control

    Advocate at the state level (licensing boards, state law) and national level (ACA, federal policy) for deliberate governance controls. Push to ensure technology adoption does not outpace statutory guidelines, ethical codes, and public knowledge.

  2. Group Initiative · Health Department Advocacy

    Influence State DoH Policy

    Directly engage state Departments of Health (e.g., WA and OR DoH) to establish enforceable clinical benchmarks, transparent data privacy mandates, and mandatory disclosure guidelines for AI tools used in healthcare settings.

  3. Scott Robertson · Legislative Transparency

    Statutory Transparency & Explainability

    Advocate for statutory provisions requiring full transparency and explainability in public-interest AI legislation, counteracting proprietary opacity and commercial black-box models in mental health software.

  4. Scott Robertson · Equity in Risk Definition

    Marginalized Community Voice

    Push for marginalized, underrepresented, and historically burdened communities to hold a decisive leadership voice in defining AI risk, establishing governance frameworks, and evaluating technological harm.

Core clinical frameworks, institutional toolkits, and annotated scholarship supporting informed AI stewardship.

Association for Counselor Education and Supervision research report on technology in counselor training.

APA AI Tool Guide (2025)

Ethical Guidance for AI in the Professional Practice of Health Service Psychology.

Arora et al. (2023) — Algorithmic Bias & Data Colonialism

Examines algorithmic bias, data colonialism, and marginalization. AI training data over-represents dominant cultures, compounding digital divides. A relational risk perspective shows risk is culturally defined; counselors must understand AI bias origins to uphold ACA C.5 Nondiscrimination.

Bates et al. (2026) — Client Perceptions & Distrust

Qualitative analysis revealing college-aged clients' trust deficits regarding AI intake and therapeutic tools. Fear of replacement and privacy anxieties impede adoption. Highlights clinician duties to disclose AI usage proactively and protect autonomy through informed consent.

Cambraia & Pyrrho (2025) — Technodigital Colonialism

Analyzes Large Language Models through historical ethics and technodigital colonialism. High-level ethical principles alone are insufficient without understanding systemic oppression. Counselors must recognize how opaque models perpetuate discrimination (ACA C.2.a, C.5).

D’Alfonso (2020) — AI Applications in Mental Health

Surveys digital phenotyping, natural language processing, and chatbots (e.g., Woebot, Wysa). Highlights that while conversational AI offers CBT access benefits, chatbots are unsuitable for crises and demand human oversight (ACA H.1.a).

Fulmer (2019) — Four Levels of AI Implementation

Outlines historical, contemporary, AGI, and superintelligence levels of AI implementation relative to counseling pillars (relationship, empowerment, goal achievement). Demonstrates that current chatbots do not fully meet therapeutic alliance standards.

Fulmer et al. (2021) — Ethics of Psychological AI

Identifies six core ethical issues in psychological AI: competence boundaries, limited ethical codes, transparency, cultural diversity, validity, and cybersecurity. Recommends continuing education and supervision (ACA C.2.a, H.1.a).

Haltaufderheide & Ranisch (2024) — LLMs in Healthcare Review

Systematic review of 53 healthcare LLM articles. Notes risks of racial bias in model outputs (e.g., ChatGPT/Foresight) and stresses mandatory human oversight to validate machine outputs across all clinical applications.

Hicks et al. (2024) — ChatGPT & Uninterested Credibility

Argues AI "hallucinations" are best characterized as Frankfurtian "bullshit"—output produced without regard for truth. Advises clinicians to reject blind trust in AI's authoritative language.

Lee & Kim (2025) — AI Literacy & Mental Health Outcomes

Scoping review demonstrating that higher AI literacy empowers users to utilize AI as an auxiliary reflection tool while critically evaluating outputs, serving as a buffer against misinformation.

Mbiazi et al. (2025) — Socio-Technical Perspective on AI Ethics

Surveys fairness, data protection, accountability, explainability, and environmental impact. Underscores the necessity of legislative advocacy for public interest transparency.

Minaee et al. (2024) — Large Language Models Survey

Comprehensive survey of LLM architectures, training data, and systemic limitations. Emphasizes that despite wide accessibility, underlying models are highly complex and prone to structural errors.

Sahoo et al. (2024) — Prompt Engineering Survey

Surveys 41 prompt engineering techniques. Demonstrates that while structured querying improves model accuracy, it cannot fully eliminate inherent algorithmic bias or factual inaccuracy.

Varghese et al. (2024) — Public Perception Survey

Evaluates public attitudes toward AI therapy tools. Shows accessibility appreciation contrasted with deep privacy and empathy concerns, reinforcing clinician duty to guide clients safely.

Yang et al. (2024) — LLMs vs. Fine-Tuned Models

Compares broadly scoped LLMs with local, fine-tuned models. Shows fine-tuned models offer lower resource demands, reduced privacy risks, and fewer bias vulnerabilities for specialized tasks.

Zhai et al. (2024) — Over-Reliance & Cognitive Abilities

Systematic review showing habitual reliance on AI dialogue systems erodes critical thinking, decision-making faculties, and communication skills, especially in trainees and students (ACA C.2.a).
